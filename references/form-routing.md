# Form

Template design starts from a layout and pours content in. Folio starts from what each unit must make the reader do, and lets that choose the form. A unit is one slide, one poster, one screen or section, one canvas frame, one page or spread.

The check: remove decoration, keeping the content, evidence, and their relationships. The reader should still be able to do what the unit is for.

## Nine forms

| Form | The unit's job | Signs | It needs | It fails when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statement | Make one sentence memorable | A single claim that can be repeated | Giant type, a full field, very few elements | A second piece of evidence or an explaining paragraph creeps in |
| Exhibit | Make one object seen clearly | A clear protagonist: a thing, a person, an interface | The protagonist on ≥ 40% of the content area, labeled directly | The protagonist shrinks to a corner or shares the stage |
| Argument | Make a judgment accepted | Claim, evidence, implication | A judgment title; evidence that can be counted | Numbers without a judgment, or evidence on a different axis from the claim |
| Sequence | Lead the reader through | Steps, time, or cause that depend on order | One axis, one action per step, a conclusion at the end | Snake columns, shuffled order, ordered items set as equals |
| Comparison | Make a difference visible | Two or more things to compare | Shared scales for comparable quantities, direct labels | Misaligned scales, unlike quantities treated as equal, color as the only difference |
| Drill | Make the reader do it | A task, space to answer, a way to check | Room to answer; answers on a separate unit | Answers on the same unit, the task squeezed by decoration |
| Parallel | Let peers be scanned | Items with no order between them | Shared alignment; unequal spans only for real priority | Invented rankings, inconsistent treatment of equals, every item stuffed full |
| Turn | Signal a new chapter | Entering a new section | One sentence about what comes next | Body text, a four-card preview, a repeated table of contents |
| Reference | Let the reader look things up | Appendix, index, glossary, schedule, data table | A visible order (alphabet, number, time, category) and headings to scan by; density is welcome | Decoration, a decisive move, or an order the reader cannot guess |

A closing unit gathers the claim or names the next action; it adds no unsupported conclusion.

## Rules of form

- One primary form per unit, with at most one subordinate move (a four-line summary in a corner of an argument). Two primary forms mean two units.
- A form may span several units (an argument continued over a spread, a reference table over many pages); each unit still has its own lead.
- Content outranks dialect. Adjust the composition and density within the shared system; reconsider the dialect if it cannot serve the task. Never bend the content to keep a style.
- Choose the dialect before extending the system; a new direction requires a coherent revision, not a different dialect on each page.

## Common misreadings

A drill set as an argument shows the answers. Parallel content with real differences loses them in an equal grid; reference content loses scanability when every row changes shape. An argument written as statements gives a deck of slogans with nothing proved. A web first screen built like a slide cover offers a slogan with no product and no next step. A poster given a deck's density cannot be read from three meters.
