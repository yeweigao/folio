# Apple + Luxury style — craft, heritage and one product

**Invocation:** Make work as a luxury house's page is made: Apple as the base, then one brand from this industry as the governing hand, chosen by what kind of luxury the input claims; craft and heritage prove the claim, one product carries the page, serif speaks where Apple would use sans, and one signature colour and no more; never a spec sheet, never chrome competing with the photograph, never a house's mark, pattern or product photograph borrowed from another house.

The luxury industry, from fashion and leather to jewellery, watches and premium beauty. What separates it from other industries is that its proof is not an interface, a figure or a price, but craft made visible: leather and stitching at detail scale, a garment worn in weather, a watch movement, a house's own history carried by a single product. Spoken in Apple, it keeps the single-accent discipline, the product as sculpture and one lead per screen, and turns the gallery into a house: restraint stays, but the display voice becomes serif, and heritage becomes the argument.

Choose this dialect when the input is fashion, leather goods, jewellery, a watch, premium beauty, or any subject whose claim is craft, heritage or a single iconic product. Then choose one brand inside it as the governing hand, by what kind of luxury it is — British heritage, craft minimalism, timeless black and white, or a digital flagship — not by which brand is most famous, and not by picking two at once. Where the input is a car or a configurator, that is Showroom, not Luxury; where it is an everyday product whose claim is subtraction, that is Minimalist, not Luxury.

- **The industry is the router; the brand is the look.** Industry names the kind of proof — craft detail, a single product in weather, a timeless photograph — and the brand hand supplies the philosophy: ground, signature colour, serif voice, button shape, chrome. A luxury dialect that names no brand has no look; a brand used without the industry's craft has no reason.
- **One governing hand per work.** The chosen brand governs the whole work; other brands in this industry visit only as single moves, one detail each. Two governing brands on one unit — Hermès craft under Burberry's check, for example — is two dialects, not one.
- **Serif is the display voice, and that is the overturn.** Apple speaks in sans; luxury speaks in serif for display — Didot, Bodoni and custom house serifs — with a quiet grotesque for labels. A sans-only luxury page has kept Apple's voice and lost the house's.
- **One signature colour, and blue retires for that work.** Each house has one colour that is its own — Hermès orange, Burberry's Knight blue, Tiffany blue — and that colour, when it governs, is the only accent in the work. Apple blue does not sit beside it; two accents is not luxury, it is chrome. The signature colour does a pill, a link or a highlight, never a field, a wash or a background.
- **Price is quiet, craft is loud.** Unlike Showroom, where price stays visible, luxury leads with craft and heritage; price stays present but quiet, never larger than the product name, never the headline. A luxury page that leads with price has mistaken a shop for a house.
- **No house mark, pattern or photograph is borrowed.** A check, a monogram, a knight, an interlocking mark, a product photograph — all belong to their house. This dialect borrows form — serif display, single product proof, one signature colour, generous whitespace — never another house's marks, patterns or images.
- **No values are specified here.** Ground, signature colour, type and button shape are drawn from the chosen brand's own knowledge, checked against that brand's philosophy below. A value that contradicts the philosophy is wrong even if it matches a remembered brand detail.

**Hands in this school.** One governs; the others visit as single moves.
- **Burberry — heritage restored.** A serif wordmark restored after a sans-serif era, a historic knight mark, a single trench coat as the product that carries the house, British weather and function and realism as the product narrative, a single saturated blue as the house colour, a house pattern treated as the product's own motif and never borrowed. Suits British heritage, outerwear and any house whose claim is restoration. Never: a sans wordmark pretending heritage does not matter, a pattern borrowed from another house, a product range with no single product to carry it.
- **Hermès — craft in minimalism.** A clean, neutral palette that lets the product shine, high-quality photography with crisp product and softened surroundings, refined typography, subtle animations, craft detail as proof, a single orange as the signature colour and the only accent in the work. Suits leather, craft and timeless products. Never: a second accent beside the orange, a crowded page, a craft claim without a detail photograph to prove it.
- **Chanel — timeless black and white.** Black and white as the foundation, clean lines, ample whitespace, classic typography, a minimalist aesthetic that lets the product lead, an interlocking mark as identity — never borrowed, only understood as the house's own. Suits timeless fashion, beauty and any house whose claim is restraint. Never: a colour field, a crowded grid, a headline that shouts louder than the product.
- **Louis Vuitton — the digital flagship.** A website treated as a digital flagship store, immersive high-resolution photography and video that show leather texture and stitching at detail scale, elegant layouts with clean lines and ample whitespace, smooth animations and interactive features that let the visitor explore collections without overwhelming them. Suits flagship ranges and houses whose claim is experience. Never: a catalogue grid pretending to be a flagship, a product without a detail image, an animation that decorates rather than reveals craft.

**Visitors, single moves only.**
- **Tiffany — one colour as identity.** A single blue that carries every primary action, search orb and rating dot, and nothing else chromatic. Never: a second colour beside it.
- **Cartier and Rolex — precision as proof.** Serif and gold and black, detail and movement photographed at a scale where precision can be judged. Never: a precision claim without a detail to judge it by.
- **Dior — cinematic serif narrative.** A serif-led, cinematic presentation where the story carries the product. Never: a cinematic page with no product detail inside it.

**Master edition rules.** Internal padding is never zero: text never touches its box edge, and a detail image clears title descenders rather than overlapping them. Cover spacing is buffered against serif substitution, because a display title that wraps must get a taller box and the body below it must move, not be overlapped. An image reused at a new crop across units is declared as reuse, not hidden as a second proof; a reused image is a second use, not a second proof.

**Alignment and accent, restated.** When a hand from this dialect governs, its single accent replaces every Apple blue role for that work, and Apple blues retire for that work. Body, leads, captions, card names and prices are flush left, ragged right, in every surface and format; only a hero, vista or cover headline may centre.

**continuous corner:** A hand may choose square forms; every nonzero radius uses Apple continuous corner. Radius sets size, not contour. Nested corners share their centre and subtract the gap from the outer radius for the inner one. Use the theme's native API or continuous corner path; plain circular rounding and a superellipse are forbidden. Write this construction into the standalone DESIGN.md.

**Apple keeps:** the single-accent discipline, the product as sculpture, one lead per screen, whitespace as divider.

**Test:** Cover the signature colour: craft, heritage and the single product still read, in that order. Swap the governing hand: the same product must look like a different house, not the same page with a colour changed.

**Caricature:** a borrowed check, monogram or knight; a second accent beside the signature colour; a signature colour used as a field or wash; a price larger than the product name; a spec sheet pretending to be a house; a sans-only display pretending serif does not matter.
