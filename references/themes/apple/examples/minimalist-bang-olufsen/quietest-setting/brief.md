## Reader and outcome

A reader who assumes a small speaker is judged by how loud it goes. The outcome: they leave believing the harder number is how quiet it can be before the room takes over — and they know the gap is two decibels, not a mood.

## The idea

On this surface, *the distance between the room's own noise and the speaker at its lowest setting* becomes *the ruler at the foot of the slide*: the claim is stated in words at the top and then measured, to scale, at the bottom, so the reader finishes the argument by reading a scale rather than a sentence.

## Sequence

1. Wordmark, top left. Nothing else in the header: this slide has one job.
2. The claim, two lines: the room is quieter than the speaker. Chinese line under it, same claim, not a translation of the mood.
3. `30 dB` at display size with its label (`lowest setting, at one metre`) and one sentence of context that names both numbers.
4. The scale: 24 to 40 dB SPL, the room at 28, the speaker at 30, and the two-decibel gap drawn as a solid bar. The scale is the proof; the sentence above it is the caption for the scale.
5. The object, drawn at one scale — 480 mm tall, 96 mm wide, so the drawing can be measured — standing on its shadow at the right, with a dimension line and the render declaration under it.

## Assumptions and gaps

- Sound Column is a fictional product of a fictional house; `30 dB`, `28 dB` and `480 mm` are invented test figures for this world, not measurements of any real device.
- Room tone varies by room; the slide states one empty room as the reference and does not generalise it.
- The product is drawn, not photographed; the caption says so, and no photograph is implied.
- The scale is linear in dB SPL with a 1 m reference; it is a reading aid, not a calibrated instrument.
