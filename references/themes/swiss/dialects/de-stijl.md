# De Stijl — relations in balance

**Invocation:** 荷兰风格派 De Stijl — Compose with orthogonal tracks and unequal primary and non-colour planes; anchor type, images and information to their relationships, then tune the balance by moving one boundary at a time. Choose the historical hand or a contemporary reading construction before drawing; never lay a black lattice over a finished page.

The Netherlands, 1917–31: Mondrian, van Doesburg, van der Leck, Huszár, Rietveld, and the magazine *De Stijl*. Its planes and tracks make relations visible. For contemporary decks and reports, choose one [reference-derived construction](../examples/de-stijl/README.md); record its additions in DESIGN.md rather than attribute them to Mondrian.

- **Planes can carry reading.** Merge cells into fields for paragraphs, images, comparisons or stages. Let text growth change the field or page count, rather than squeeze the body into small rectangles. Constructed lettering belongs to display roles.
- **The drawn grid organizes the page.** Bars follow reading axes, text edges and section boundaries. They may continue beyond the trim; a chosen contemporary construction may use open brackets or local patches. Keep track and bar-weight relationships recognizable as plane spans and occupied/empty intervals vary within a page and across the deck.
- **Balance the whole by opposition.** Answer vertical with horizontal, colour with non-colour, large with small. Equal columns, cells and intervals remain available for genuine peers; a table's inner rules stay distinct from the composition's heavy bars.
- **Colour follows the chosen construction.** Mondrian's compositions use flat primaries against white, grey and black. A contemporary world resolves its own grounds, calibrated hues and type roles; circles, photographs or warm paper belong only to the construction that specifies them. No compulsory three-primary quota on every page.

**Hands in this school.** One governs; the others visit as single moves.
- **Piet Mondrian — the compositions (1921–30).** Place horizontal and vertical bars, then move one bar or resize one plane at a time until the counterweights hold. Tape on canvas becomes re-rendered tracks and flat planes. Suits abstract relationships and unequal weights. Never: curves or depicted objects in this governing method.
- **Piet Mondrian — *Broadway Boogie Woogie* (1942–43).** Replace black bars with chains of small primary and grey squares; vary their intervals to make the rhythm. Suits turns, closers and urban sequences. Never: a second black lattice beside the chains.
- **Theo van Doesburg — the counter-compositions (1924–26).** Turn the organizing axes to 45° against the frame; balance flat planes along those axes for directional tension. Suits rupture and arguments in motion. Never: slip this diagonal into a Mondrian-governed unit.
- **Bart van der Leck — the Batavier Line poster and *Mine Triptych* (1916).** Break one subject figure into separate primary and black bars on white until its gesture remains. Suits people and objects reduced to signs. Never: a contour drawn around the bars.

**Visitors, single moves only.**
- **Gerrit Rietveld — the Red-Blue Chair (1918–23) and Schröder House (1924).** Let bars overrun crossings and planes slide past the joint. Never: an overrun that crosses nothing.
- **Vilmos Huszár and Theo van Doesburg — the *De Stijl* masthead (1917) and alphabet (1919).** Build a short display word from rectangles on a square grid, with strokes related to the bars. Never: carry this alphabet into paragraph text.

**Swiss keeps:** shared tracks, flush-left reading fields, the type hierarchy and the facts. Faces may differ by role; one sans for every word is not compulsory.

**Test:** Moving one plane changes the whole balance. In a reading world, DESIGN.md alone must support new paragraphs, a comparison or table, and a sequence while preserving the construction; shared colours alone do not establish continuity.

**Caricature:** random primary cards; the same black border on every slide; body squeezed into leftover cells; circles added merely because the brief says Mondrian; a display alphabet used as body; decoration mistaken for data.
