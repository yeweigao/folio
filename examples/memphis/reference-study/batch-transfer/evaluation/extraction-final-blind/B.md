# A tiled, brightly coloured art identity

## Evidence and scope

This system is extracted from one identity board containing a wide composition, narrow printed strips, cards, bags and hanging tags. The pieces share a vocabulary but rearrange it considerably. No author, exact colour specifications, production dimensions or original font identities are established. The palette below is a visual approximation. The available licensed fonts are substitutes, not identifications of the reference fonts.

The identity is the relationship between a quiet white or blue field, dense square-edged display lettering, and a tightly joined construction of colourful geometric tiles. Its Memphis character comes from that particular construction. Do not substitute an assortment of unrelated squiggles, speckles and floating shapes.

## Field and structure

- Use a flat white or saturated medium cobalt-blue ground. White versions carry black and blue text; blue versions carry white text. The blue is simultaneously a background colour, a major tile colour and a display-text colour.
- Organise the graphic material on a square or rectangular module. Let `u` be a convenient principal tile width; half-width columns and shallow strips may subdivide it. Tiles meet without gutters. Their outer edges remain horizontal and vertical, even when their contents are circular.
- Build a connected assembly, usually rooted at the bottom edge, sometimes climbing one or both sides in unequal steps. Its silhouette resembles a low irregular skyline. Leave large areas of ground above or beside it for text. Do not distribute equal numbers of ornaments evenly around the whole perimeter.
- Internal boundaries align: a curve ends at a tile edge, a strip meets the next rectangle, triangles share a baseline, and adjacent modules form uninterrupted rows. A graphic assembly should read as one constructed object before its individual motifs are noticed.
- Allow a few independent marks in the empty field immediately above the assembly. These are specific companion motifs, not general confetti. They can punctuate an open interval, point toward the construction or rise from its roofline.
- Everything is crisp, flat and smooth-edged. Use no shadows, bevels, gradients, paper texture or hand-drawn contours. Display letters can have stepped outlines while the graphic curves remain smooth.

The grey surround and platform watermark of the supplied board are presentation furniture, not identity material. Handles, straps and barcodes belong to the particular objects shown; include their functional equivalents only when the new format needs them.

Use large blocks of blue and orange-red to carry the graphic mass. Balance them with pale pink and blue, then add yellow and red as concentrated accents. Black is chiefly typographic, with a small but distinctive role in the dotted flower and hollow circles. It is not a general outline around every tile. An off-white or pale cream can be used for light arc bands within warm tiles.

Avoid turning every motif into a full-palette illustration. Most individual modules use two or three colours. The larger assembly supplies the variety.

## Motif construction

### Broad circular tiles

These are architectural building blocks, not little icons floating inside padded cards.

- **Inset half-disk:** place a circle centre on a tile's left or right boundary, clipping away half the circle. Give the visible semicircle a broad contrasting rim, with an inset disk of another colour. The rim is a substantial colour area, approximately one sixth to one quarter of the radius, not a fine outline. Useful combinations include blue inside orange-red and pale blue inside middle blue. A mirrored pair can occupy adjacent tiles, each half-disk attached to an outside edge and swelling toward their central join. Pink or cream fills the remaining corners.
- **Quarter disk:** crop a large disk to one corner of a square or rectangle. The curved area occupies much of the tile. Blue/orange-red and blue/blush combinations establish the characteristic warm-cool balance. Some modules can put a pale curved region over a darker field without a rim.
- **Quarter-circle bands:** draw three or four concentric curved bands from a common tile corner. Clip all bands at the tile boundaries. Keep their thicknesses and intervening gaps consistent. Use pale bands on orange-red, orange-red bands on blush, or blue bands on orange-red. Rotating the corner changes their direction without changing their construction. These are open quarter rings, not a set of full concentric circles centred inside a square.
- **Divided disk:** a large circle can fill nearly a whole square, split into blue and yellow by one diagonal through its centre. A much smaller orange-red disk sits at that centre. Retain the straight diagonal, circular outer boundary and small central hub.
- **Rounded block:** a broad red rectangle can have opposite corners deeply rounded, leaving the other corners square, against a pink field. Its height, straight edges and large-radius corners make it part of the tile construction rather than a loose blob.

### Two distinct flowers

Retain their different anatomy. A generic daisy cannot replace both.

- **Red dotted rosette:** six broad rounded red lobes form a compact, approximately circular flower. Place six solid black circular dots around the perimeter, one within each lobe, at a common distance from the centre. A small yellow scalloped disk occupies the middle. The black dots are substantial and evenly spaced; no stem, outline or detached petals. This flower often fills a pale or white square tile with little spare room, but can also stand above a graphic band.
- **Golden overlapping flower:** four rounded yellow lobes create a soft, almost square clover silhouette. Overlapping inner orange and golden petal areas create a warmer cross-like centre and layered lobes. A small pale four-lobed centre holds a tiny orange-red circular dot. Use discrete flat colour shapes to suggest overlap, not a radial gradient. This flower is often an isolated focal accent on open ground, with more breathing room than the red rosette.

### Angular and small companion marks

- **Triangle seam:** assemble a shallow strip of touching isosceles triangles, sharing edges and a common row height. Alternate structural blue, middle blue and pale blue, with occasional blush or yellow. Their peaks can meet the bottom of larger tiles. This is an angular foundation or connecting seam.
- **Sawtooth group:** use a short run of orange-red right triangles, all with upright left edges, sloping down to the right and sharing one baseline. Their tips rise into the open field above a lower band. Keep them in a small ordered run.
- **Two-tone diamond:** join a blue upward triangle and a pale-blue downward triangle along their horizontal bases. Together they form a vertically oriented diamond, detached from the construction when used as a small accent.
- **Blue starburst:** a solid blue burst with roughly a dozen long, narrow triangular rays. No central hole, outline or extra colour. It can occupy an otherwise empty interval above the tiles.
- **Petal junction:** use two or three rounded teardrop or quarter-ellipse shapes meeting at one point, often where tiles intersect. Blue, light blue and orange-red distinguish the leaves. A large blue semicircular area can shelter an orange leaf, with a smaller pale-blue leaf outside it. Keep the common junction and smooth arcs; do not add a realistic stem.
- **Hollow-circle cluster:** six identical open circles arranged as two rows of three. Use black on white or white on blue. Their thin, consistent strokes and generous internal holes contrast with the solid tiles. Keep the group compact, without turning it into a full-background dot pattern.
- **Overlapping circle:** a small orange-red circle can straddle a blue rectangle's edge. Its portion over blue may be middle blue, creating a deliberately split or overprinted appearance. A nearby shallow blue band can repeat that colour. This is a local overlap treatment, not an instruction to apply transparency everywhere.
- **Stripe bars:** repeat narrow equal-width vertical orange-red and yellow bands in a short horizontal strip. A different, extremely narrow upright edge strip alternates blue/light blue and pink. The latter can run alongside a tall stack as a fine terminal accent. Keep these bars attached to tile edges.
- **Raised rounded cap:** an orange-red upright rectangle with one upper corner rounded into a large quarter-circle can rise above an edge stack. Its bottom joins the tiles; its outer vertical edge remains straight.

Use the flowers, circular tiles, arc bands and stripes as recurrent identity anchors. Select and repeat from the vocabulary rather than putting every motif into every piece. Isolated marks remain a minority relative to joined modules.

## Typography and licensed materials

The principal lettering is heavy, compact and conspicuously stepped: squared Chinese forms and broad blocky Latin capitals, with small stair-step cuts or protrusions at some corners and terminals. Small detached square marks occasionally sit near headline punctuation. These are restrained extensions of the letter geometry, not a pixel pattern applied to the whole layout.

Use `pixel.woff2` for Latin display lettering where its authored surrogate forms are suitable. It is supplied under SIL OFL 1.1 and is not the unidentified original font. Use `cjk.woff2` as the available CJK substitute, matching its visual weight and scale to the display lettering. If ordinary CJK outlines are too smooth, custom stepped display outlines may be needed for a close match; do not claim the fallback alone reproduces the source. Preserve counters, character recognition and consistent strokes when adjusting forms.

Use `sans-400.woff2` for small explanatory text, `sans-700.woff2` for supporting labels and `sans-900.woff2` for compact numeric emphasis. Their metadata identifies the Inter project and SIL Open Font License 1.1. The supplied CJK file is also OFL licensed. The neutral inventory additionally provides `light.woff2` and `condensed.woff2`; they are available materials, but this board does not establish them as identity requirements.

Display copy is tightly set, with short, deliberate line breaks. Chinese generally has the largest character height. Latin is uppercase and bold, sometimes broad enough to occupy a comparable block width over more lines. Small prose is conventional, compact sans serif with clear line spacing. Numeric information can be bold and narrow in footprint. Thin horizontal rules may separate prose and practical information on long strips.

Keep the supplied brand signature, if the new content has one, modest in scale and separate from the headline. The observed small signature has softer letterforms than the headline; do not infer that all typography must be pixelated.

## Recomposition by format

- **Wide white field:** allow a large open upper and central area. Root the joined graphic along the bottom and let side stacks rise to different heights. A bilingual headline can occupy two adjacent blocks separated by a thin blue vertical rule, with each language broken according to its own length. This is one supported arrangement, not a mandatory position or line count. Small identity and supporting copy can use remote upper corners.
- **White information card:** reserve one side for short text groups with generous separation, and use a tightly cropped vertical graphic stack on the opposite edge. Keep most of the card white. A single flower within the stack supplies concentrated emphasis.
- **Blue poster or card:** use white display text in the upper or central open area, with smaller supporting language below. The graphic usually gathers into a low band or bottom corner and may climb a side. The blue ground can merge seamlessly with blue graphic tiles; pale and warm areas make its boundary legible.
- **Tall narrow strip:** graphics may occupy approximately the upper half as large, vertically stacked modules. Centre the headline and supporting language below, then separate small practical information toward the foot. A blue lower field reverses the typography to white. Do not miniaturise a wide scene to fit the strip: enlarge and crop a few distinctive tiles instead.
- **Bag or hanging tag:** the same vocabulary can become a broad middle band, an upper corner wrapping down one side, or a compact base assembly. Let the format determine whether the text sits above, below or inside an open blue interval. Do not preserve the exact poster arrangement on every object.

Across formats, preserve the scale contrast: large joined geometry, an assertive but compact title block, small orderly information, and substantial quiet ground. Reflow the title, change the graphic assembly's depth, crop or rotate circular modules, vary which edge it occupies and omit secondary motifs as needed. Preserve each motif's internal relationships and the assembly's flush joins. There is no evidence for one compulsory tile order, universal border, fixed title location or fixed ratio of image to text.

## Handoff check

A new piece should be recognisable through its blue/orange-red structural pairing, pale curved inserts, corner-centred arc bands, distinctive flowers, joined modular edges and stepped display lettering. Check that it still reads as one composed graphic construction when viewed small. If it instead reads as scattered Memphis stickers, add structure through shared boundaries and reduce detached decoration. If the construction overwhelms the information, open a larger continuous white or blue field before reducing all elements uniformly.
