# Bauhaus — elements as forces

**Invocation:** 包豪斯 Bauhaus — Make work as the Bauhaus built from Kandinsky's elements: on the basic plane (the format itself) set point, line and the planar forms (circle, square, triangle, and the bar and halves the Vorkurs added) as forces, in red, yellow, blue and black, until they build the subject's figure; never shapes that build nothing.

Weimar 1919, Dessau 1925–32: Kandinsky's *Point and Line to Plane* (1926), Itten's and Albers' Vorkurs, Schmidt's 1923 exhibition poster, Bayer's universal alphabet, Moholy-Nagy's transparent planes and typophoto; its typography codified in Tschichold's "Elementare Typographie" (1925); visited from Vitebsk and Moscow by Suprematism and Constructivism. What separates it from De Stijl is not the diagonal (van Doesburg broke with Mondrian to use it) but the building: De Stijl balances planes that depict nothing, the Bauhaus constructs a figure, a letter, an object. Spoken in Swiss, the Bauhaus builds the image itself from elements.

- **Elements are forces on the plane, not ornament.** The point is the smallest circle, at rest; the line is a point in motion and carries a direction; circle, square and triangle are planes that weigh. Each is placed for what it does in the figure; the figure belongs to this subject: a face, a machine, a letter, a building, a number.
- **Direction is meaning (Kandinsky).** Horizontal is calm, vertical active, the diagonal tension. A unit runs on verticals and horizontals while its content rests, and turns to one diagonal family (parallels and their right angles) when its content moves, rises or clashes.
- **Colour corresponds to form, and mixes only where parts meet.** Red, yellow and blue with black, by Kandinsky's law or by order. Two planes overlap only where two parts of the content truly share something; the overlap shows their mixture as a transparency (Moholy-Nagy's planes, Albers' illusion of transparency), made by choosing the colour between the two, never by computing a multiply: red and yellow give orange, yellow and blue green, red and blue violet, black stays black. No mix appears anywhere else.
- **Type is constructed and asymmetric.** A geometric sans, or letters and numerals built from the same elements; bars and rules set the reading order; flush left, across, vertical or along the diagonal; Latin may go lowercase.

**Hands in this school.** One governs; the others visit as single moves.
- **Joost Schmidt — the Bauhaus exhibition poster (1923).** Start from the subject's figure and draw it with compass and square from the elements on one module until it reads; then set the type into the same construction. Suits events and institutions where one figure can stand for the subject. Never: shapes that depict nothing.
- **Wassily Kandinsky — *Point and Line to Plane* (1926).** Compose with the elements as forces and give form and colour by law (yellow triangle, red square, blue circle) to three real parts of the subject. Suits music, rhythm, abstract subjects. Never: the three forms recited, constructing nothing.
- **Herbert Bayer — the universal alphabet (1925) and the poster for Kandinsky's 60th birthday (1926).** Make the typography the structure: bars, rules and circles organize lowercase type into an asymmetric reading order. Suits programmes and information with a lot of type. Never: capitals for ceremony.
- **Josef Albers — the combination alphabet (Kombinationsschrift, 1926).** Build letters and numerals from square, quarter circle and circle on a sub-grid of the module, so title and figures are made of the image's parts. Suits titles, numbers, identities. Never: a borrowed "Bauhaus" display face standing in for construction.

**Visitors, single moves only.**
- **László Moholy-Nagy — typophoto, the Bauhausbücher (1925).** Photograph, type and bars in one construction, in diagonal tension. Never: a static centred emblem.
- **Kazimir Malevich and El Lissitzky — the Suprematist compositions (1915–) and *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge* (1919).** Planes float along one diagonal on an open ground; one wedge may cut one circle. Never: a second diagonal family.

**A fourth part takes the line.** A fourth term (sound, time, a sequence) is drawn in black line, never in a fourth hue.

**Swiss keeps:** the module, every shape snapped to one square grid at doubling sizes (on a diagonal unit, the grid turns with it); reading text horizontal; the type ramp; the facts.

**Test:** Remove the type, and the shapes still build a figure of this subject; every overlap names something two parts share; the direction matches whether the content rests or moves.

**Caricature:** the Kandinsky triad recited on any subject; primaries floating on a ground, building nothing; overlaps for prettiness, or mixed colours as flat fills; diagonals on content at rest; every shape at its own angle; a borrowed "Bauhaus" display face; pastel or teal "Bauhaus"; fake halftone or grain; gradients, shadows, rounded cards.
